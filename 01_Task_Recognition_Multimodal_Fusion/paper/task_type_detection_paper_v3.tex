\begin{abstract}
Robots that assist human operators in industrial settings must adapt to what the
operator is doing. One source of evidence for the operator's current state is their
own physiology: brain and peripheral signals change systematically across cognitive,
affective, and physical workload. In this paper we study whether the \emph{type} of
task an operator is performing can be recognized from such signals in a way that
generalizes to operators never seen during training. We work with the public
MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} dataset (52 operators, 21 protocol conditions, 5,640
windows), which we clean into a leakage-free representation of 128 statistical
features (\ac{EEG} and peripheral channels). Labels are defined as five
\emph{task-condition} classes---cognitive load, high stress, industrial task, low
load, and other---derived deterministically from each recording's protocol. We then
evaluate eleven classifiers under a strictly subject-disjoint \ac{LOSO} protocol in
which all preprocessing is refit inside every training fold. Headline results are
modest and honest: the best detector (gradient boosting) reaches a per-fold
macro-F1 of $0.466\pm0.149$ across the 52 held-out operators---far below the
$\sim$0.91 macro-F1 that a row-level (within-subject) split reports on the same
features. We attribute this generalization gap to per-operator physiological
signatures and quantify it: an assistance system that meets an operator for the
first time, without calibration data, stands to lose roughly 0.45 in macro-F1
relative to a model that has already seen that operator. A modality ablation shows
fused \ac{EEG}+peripheral features consistently and significantly outperform the
best single modality (peripheral physiology), while an explicit two-tower fusion
network reaches 0.406, below fused \ac{XGBoost} (0.462). All detectors classify a
window in at most $\sim$90\,$\mu$s, well under the sensor window, confirming
real-time feasibility. We release the cleaning protocol,
feature manifest, and evaluation scripts so the benchmark is reproducible.
\keywords{physiological signals, task-type recognition, multimodal fusion, human-robot collaboration, subject-disjoint evaluation, reproducibility}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In shared and assistive \ac{HRC}, a robot and an operator work on the same physical
task and must coordinate in real time. Coordinating well requires the robot to
interpret not only the geometric state of the shared workspace but also the state of
the person working in it: whether they are under high mental load, stressed, or
performing a demanding physical manipulation changes what kind of assistance is
useful and how it should be timed \cite{selvaggio2021autonomy}. Physiological
signals provide a continuous, non-disruptive window onto these states---brain
activity through \ac{EEG}, and autonomic arousal through \ac{ECG}, \ac{EDA},
\ac{EMG}, and respiration---and are increasingly used to drive adaptive behaviour in
collaborative robotics \cite{bussolan2024stressdetector,borghi2025operatorstress}.

The task we address is \emph{task-state recognition}: given a window of physiological
signals, decide which kind of activity the operator is engaged in. We deliberately
use coarse, task-condition classes (a cognitive-load battery, a stressor, an
industrial physical task, rest, and a residual class) rather than fine-grained
continuous states, because coarse classes are the actionable unit for an assistive
policy and because they are what the recording protocol can certify without invasive
labelling. Yet even this formulation is hard in practice. Two properties dominate:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operator dependence.} Physiological baselines and response magnitudes
differ sharply between individuals. A model trained on windows from a set of
operators can appear excellent on a random held-out \emph{subset of the same
operators' windows} while failing on a genuinely new operator. Any evaluation that
does not separate operators risks reporting a ceiling rather than a generalization
result.
\item \textbf{Class imbalance.} In the data we use, one class (industrial task)
accounts for 63\% of windows while two minority classes together account for 5\%.
Aggregate accuracy is therefore uninformative without class-level controls.
\end{itemize}

A recurring failure mode in the applied physiological-recognition literature is
exactly the first property: repeated, auto-correlated windows from the same operator
and the same condition are split randomly between train and test, and metadata such
as recording identifiers leak into the feature matrix. The resulting numbers are
flattering and irreproducible. This paper is, in part, a corrective study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textbf{leakage-free feature-cleaning protocol} for the public
MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} dataset: we remove 112 columns that are recording metadata or
duplicated \ac{EEG} exports, and publish a machine-readable manifest
(feature $\rightarrow$ modality group $\rightarrow$ keep/drop $\rightarrow$ reason),
leaving 128 unique physiological features. %D2/D3
\item A \textbf{strictly subject-disjoint \ac{LOSO} benchmark} of eleven classical and
neural detectors over all 52 operators, with preprocessing refit inside each training
fold, per-fold score files, paired significance tests, and calibration reporting. We
keep a row-level (within-subject) run on identical features only as an explicit
upper bound. %D1, D5
\item A \textbf{modality-contribution analysis} under this protocol---\ac{EEG}-only,
peripheral-only, and fused inputs---answering, under a controlled design, whether
fusion of central and peripheral physiology actually helps. %D9, R3.3/R4.5
\item A quantitative statement of the \textbf{generalization gap} between within-subject
and across-operator performance, with real-time feasibility (inference latency), a
discussion of deployment and privacy, and a released reproducibility package. %R1.1, R3.4, R3.9
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the dataset and the label schema.
Section~\ref{sec:method} details the cleaning, models, and the \ac{LOSO} evaluation

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Multimodal fusion.}
Baltrusaitis et al.\ provide the standard taxonomy of multimodal machine learning,
arguing that heterogeneous streams---here central and peripheral physiology---carry
complementary information and that naive fusion strategies can hurt when modalities
are redundant or noisy \cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Our study contributes a
controlled \emph{empirical} test of that claim on physiological \ac{HRC} data, where
redundancy across channels is high and the fusion benefit is not guaranteed.

\paragraph{Physiological operator-state recognition in \ac{HRC}.}
The field most directly related to ours is operator stress and cognitive-load
recognition in human--robot collaboration. Bussolan et al.\ introduced the
MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} dataset used here and reported subject-disjoint recognition of
self-reported stress and cognitive-load levels on an industrial assembly task,
reaching macro-F1 ranges of roughly 0.34--0.39 (stress) and 0.33--0.49
(cognitive load) across conditions
\cite{bussolan2025multiphysiohrc,bussolan2024stressdetector}. These modest
numbers---obtained with questionnaire
ground truth under a subject-disjoint protocol---are an important reference point:
they indicate that physiological operator-state recognition across operators is
genuinely hard. We differ in three ways. First, we recognize \emph{task-condition}
classes defined by the protocol rather than self-reported state, which makes our
labels deterministic and exactly reproducible. Second, we add a leakage audit of the
feature export and a modality-contribution analysis, which the source paper does not
provide. Third, we report a broad multi-model comparison instead of a single fused
architecture.

Borghi et al.\ assessed operator stress in collaborative robotics with a multimodal
approach and likewise stress careful subject-independent evaluation
\cite{borghi2025operatorstress}. Aygun et al.\ estimated cognitive workload for
assistive robots from physiological signals, again observing large between-subject
variability \cite{aygun2022assistive}. Iarlori et al.\ survey cognitive-workload
estimation from wearables in human--machine interaction and catalog the evaluation
heterogeneity that makes studies hard to compare \cite{iarlori2024cognitiveworkload}.
Jin et al.\ provide a recent systematic review of physiological cognitive-state
recognition across application domains and emphasize that dataset split design is
the single largest driver of reported performance differences \cite{jin2025cognitivestate}.
Our work directly addresses that finding by making the split design---subject-disjoint
\ac{LOSO}---the central methodological commitment.

\paragraph{Autonomy in physical \ac{HRC}.}
Selvaggio et al.\ survey autonomy levels in physical human--robot interaction and
identify reliability, transparency, and adaptation as the constraints any state
estimator must serve \cite{selvaggio2021autonomy}. Task-state recognition is a
building block of such autonomy, and our latency and failure-mode analysis speaks to
the deployment requirements they enumerate.

\paragraph{Gap and position.}
Prior work thus establishes (i) that fusion of physiological modalities is promising
in principle \cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}, (ii) that operator-state recognition
in \ac{HRC} is hard and subject-dependent
\cite{bussolan2025multiphysiohrc,bussolan2024stressdetector,borghi2025operatorstress,aygun2022assistive},
and (iii)
that evaluation design determines reported performance
\cite{jin2025cognitivestate,iarlori2024cognitiveworkload}. What is missing is a
compact, reproducible,
subject-disjoint benchmark of task-condition recognition on a public \ac{HRC} dataset
that simultaneously audits the features, tests whether fusion helps, and quantifies
the within-subject versus across-operator gap. This paper provides that benchmark.

\section{Dataset and Task Formulation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC}}

We use the public MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} dataset of multimodal physiological recordings
during controlled and realistic industrial human--robot collaboration
\cite{bussolan2025multiphysiohrc}. It comprises 52 healthy operators who each
performed a battery of protocol conditions (21 conditions in total) while wearing a
multi-sensor setup: an \ac{EEG} headset, plus peripheral sensors for \ac{ECG},
\ac{EDA}, \ac{EMG}, and respiration. From the continuous recordings the dataset
publishes fixed-duration signal windows together with statistical descriptors. The
export we obtained contains 5,640 windows, each originally carrying 257 columns.

\subsection{Auditing the feature export}

Before any modelling we audited the exported feature table for the two most common
leakage sources in this kind of data:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Recording-metadata leakage.} Sixteen columns aggregate the recording
\emph{identifier} (\texttt{ID\_\_*}) and \emph{trial counter} (\texttt{Repetition\_\_*}).
These identify the recording session and its order, not the operator's physiology;
they are removed (they would otherwise let a model short-circuit on trial metadata).
\item \textbf{Duplicated \ac{EEG} features.} The export contains each \ac{EEG}
descriptor twice: once under a clean name and once under a
\texttt{eeg\_features\_5s\_\_...} prefix, byte-identical. The 96 duplicated columns
are removed so that \ac{EEG} is weighted exactly once. %D3
\end{itemize}

The audit is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:audit}. After cleaning, the 128 remaining
physiological features are all statistical descriptors (mean, standard deviation,
median, energy, etc.) of windowed signals. A machine-readable manifest recording the
decision and reason for every column is released with the reproducibility package.

\subsection{Task-condition labels}

MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} annotates each recording with the \emph{protocol condition} that
was performed and with self-report questionnaires. The questionnaires are not part of
the public feature export we obtained, so we do not claim self-reported affective
state. Instead we define the recognition target as the \emph{task condition}, which
is certified by the experimental protocol itself: each window receives exactly one of
five labels, assigned deterministically from the recording's \texttt{task\_name} by
the mapping in Table~\ref{tab:schema} (the rule is released verbatim in the
reproducibility package).

The five classes are chosen so that every protocol condition in the battery maps to a
condition class with a coherent physiological hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{cognitive load} (975 windows): sustained-attention and memory battery
(Stroop easy/hard, N-back, Tower of Hanoi, and an arithmetic/puzzle condition). These
conditions tax working memory and executive control.
\item \textbf{high stress} (224 windows): a virtual-reality height-plank walking task,
a validated stressor inducing threat-related autonomic arousal.
\item \textbf{industrial task} (3,571 windows): ten physical assembly/order-picking
conditions, five performed manually and five with a collaborative robot. These mix
physical and cognitive demands in realistic \ac{HRC} settings.
\item \textbf{low load} (799 windows): rest and guided-meditation segments between
conditions, providing a low-demand baseline.
\item \textbf{other} (71 windows): a single open-ended virtual-reality job-simulation
session that belongs to none of the controlled batteries. It is kept as its own class
rather than forced into another, and its small size is reported explicitly.
\end{itemize}

Because labels are defined by the protocol rather than by physiology alone, the task
is \emph{well-posed but not trivial}: the five classes correspond to different
conditions, yet physiological differences across individuals are large enough that a
classifier must separate \emph{classes} from \emph{persons}. This is precisely the
separation a subject-disjoint protocol enforces (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Pipeline overview}

\subsection{Problem formulation}

Let $\{x_i, y_i, s_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote the cleaned windows, where
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the fused feature vector ($d=128$), $y_i \in
\mathcal{Y} = \{1,\dots,5\}$ is the task-condition label
(cognitive load $=1$, high stress $=2$, industrial task $=3$, low load $=4$,
other $=5$), and $s_i \in \{1,\dots,52\}$ is the operator who produced the window.
Windows of the same operator are not independent, and repeated windows of the same
condition are strongly autocorrelated.

For a model $f_\theta$ producing class posteriors $p_\theta(y=c \mid x_i)$, we train
with the class-weighted cross-entropy objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}
\sum_{c=1}^{5} w_c\,\mathbf{1}[y_i=c]\;\log p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is a training batch (the full set for batch models), and
$w_c \propto N / (5\,N_c)$ is the inverse-frequency weight of class $c$ with
$N_c=\sum_i \mathbf{1}[y_i=c]$. The weights counteract the imbalance of
Table~\ref{tab:schema} so that the loss is not dominated by the majority
\emph{industrial task} class; without them, a trivial constant classifier already
attains 63\% accuracy. The decision rule at test time is the maximum-posterior rule
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i \;=\; \arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{Y}}\; p_\theta(y=c \mid x_i),
\label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}
which, together with the class weighting in Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}, targets
class-balanced behaviour rather than raw accuracy.

\subsection{Preprocessing and feature representation}

Preprocessing is deliberately minimal and identical across models:
\begin{enumerate}
\item columns audited as in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}; the 128 kept features form
$x_i$;
\item missing values are imputed with the training-fold median (the audit found 328
missing entries out of $5{,}640\times128$);
\item features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using training-fold
statistics only; and
\item a fused representation is used by all tabular detectors, while the
modality-feature groups (\ac{EEG}, peripheral) are exposed to the ablation and to the
two-tower architecture.
\end{enumerate}
Crucially, imputation and standardization are \emph{refit inside every training fold}
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); test operators never contribute to the statistics that
transform their own windows, which prevents a subtle and common form of leakage.

\subsection{Detectors}

We compare eleven off-the-shelf and lightweight detectors so that the result is a
property of the representation and protocol rather than of one model family:
logistic regression (class-weighted), $k$-nearest neighbours, decision tree, random
forest, extra-trees, gradient boosting, histogram gradient boosting,
\ac{XGBoost} \cite{chen2016xgboost}, linear \ac{SVM}, Gaussian naive Bayes, and an
\ac{MLP}, implemented with scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Tree ensembles
and boosting are natural baselines for tabular physiological features; \ac{XGBoost} hyperparameters are fixed a priori ($400$ trees, depth $6$,
learning rate $0.05$, column and row subsampling $0.9$) rather than tuned on the test
operators.

In addition we evaluate one neural fusion architecture, \emph{two-tower}, which makes
the modality split explicit rather than concatenating features: a peripheral tower
and an \ac{EEG} tower each map their feature group to a 64-dimensional embedding
(two linear--ReLU--dropout layers), the two embeddings are concatenated, and a
two-layer head produces the five logits. Two-tower is trained with Adam (learning
rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), class-weighted cross-entropy
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}, and early stopping on a held-out validation slice inside each
training fold. It is the only detector with learnable modality structure; comparing
it with fused-tabular \ac{XGBoost} answers whether explicit fusion architecture adds
anything over plain concatenation.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The evaluation answers one question: \emph{how well does a detector trained on known
operators recognize the task state of a new operator?} We therefore use \ac{LOSO}
cross-validation over the 52 operators. Each operator in turn forms the test fold;
the detector and the preprocessing are fitted on the remaining 51 operators' windows
only. This yields 52 per-operator evaluations, whose scores are aggregated.

Within one fold the test windows come from a single operator and are heavily
autocorrelated, so the per-fold score is an estimate for that operator, not a
collection of independent samples. We therefore treat the 52 per-fold scores as the
primary unit of analysis and report their mean and standard deviation, a 95\%
confidence interval of the mean (Student $t$ over folds), and the pooled
\ac{OOF} metrics obtained by concatenating every operator's out-of-fold predictions.

\paragraph{Metrics over a fixed label space.}
Because a fold may contain no window of a minority class, per-fold scores must be
computed over the \emph{fixed} five-class space, with a missing class contributing a
recall of zero; averaging only over the classes present in a fold would make scores
incomparable across folds. Let the confusion matrix in fold $f$ be $C^f \in
\mathbb{N}^{5\times5}$. Class recall is
$r^f_c = C^f_{cc}\,/\,\max(\sum_{c'} C^f_{cc'},\,1)$,
and the balanced accuracy is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BA}_f \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5} r^f_c,
\label{eq:ba}
\end{equation}
which is the average recall (Brodersen et al. define balanced accuracy as mean
recall \cite{brodersen2010balanced}). The macro-F1 is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MacroF}_f \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5} \mathrm{F1}(c),
\qquad
\mathrm{F1}(c)=\frac{2\,p_c\,r_c}{p_c+r_c},
\label{eq:macro}
\end{equation}
where $p_c,r_c$ are the class precision and recall in the fixed space and a class
absent from both prediction and truth has $\mathrm{F1}(c)=0$ \cite{sokolova2009systematic}.
Macro-F1 is our primary ranking metric because it penalizes both precision and recall
per class and weighs all five classes equally despite the 63\%/4\%/1\% imbalance
\cite{jeni2013imbalanced}. We additionally report pooled balanced accuracy, pooled
accuracy, and, for probabilistic models, the expected calibration error (ECE)
\cite{guo2017calibration} of the pooled \ac{OOF} probabilities, which quantifies how
well a detector's confidence tracks its correctness---needed if downstream assistance
will gate on confidence.

\paragraph{Comparisons and upper bound.}
Every detector is compared with the best per-fold macro-F1 model by a paired
Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the 52 folds, with the mean difference and a paired
effect size reported. We also run a \emph{within-subject} evaluation on the identical
cleaned features: a stratified random 80/20 split in which the same operators appear
in both train and test. This is explicitly \emph{not} a claim of generalization; it
measures an optimistic ceiling in which the model has already met the operator, and
its contrast with the \ac{LOSO} result quantifies the generalization gap.

All random seeds are fixed; per-fold, per-class, and pooled result files are written
for every model and released. Inference latency is measured as per-window CPU
prediction time of each fitted detector.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main benchmark under \ac{LOSO}}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} reports all eleven detectors plus the two-tower network
under the subject-disjoint \ac{LOSO} protocol. Several observations stand out.

\input{tables/tbl_benchmark}

First, absolute performance is far below what a row-level split reports on the same
features: the best detector (gradient boosting) reaches a per-fold macro-F1 of
$0.466 \pm 0.149$ (95\% CI $[0.42,\,0.51]$) over the 52 held-out operators, with
\ac{XGBoost} statistically tied ($0.462 \pm 0.148$, paired $p = 0.88$) and the rest
of the field trailing by 0.02--0.13. Second, the ranking is dominated by tree
ensembles; linear models, $k$NN and naive Bayes trail clearly, indicating that the
class boundaries in the statistical feature space are not linearly separable. Third,
pooled balanced accuracy is consistently well below pooled accuracy (e.g.\ gradient
boosting: accuracy 0.69 vs.\ balanced accuracy 0.47), confirming that the majority
class inflates accuracy and that the class-level metrics of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ba}--\eqref{eq:macro} are the ones to trust. Fourth, calibration is
uneven rather than uniformly poor: the best detector is well calibrated
(\ac{ECE} $0.03$) but the other strong models range from 0.13 to 0.45
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), so confidence-thresholded assistance would need
model-specific recalibration before deployment.

The pooled \ac{OOF} confusion matrix of the best model
(Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows where errors concentrate, and
Table~\ref{tab:perclass} gives the corresponding class-level metrics. The four
substantial classes are recovered with \ac{F1} between 0.54 and 0.85, ordered as
\emph{industrial task} (0.85) $\gg$ \emph{high stress} (0.59) $>$
\emph{cognitive load} (0.55) $\approx$ \emph{low load} (0.54). The \emph{other}
class---71 windows drawn from a single protocol condition---is never recovered: its
windows scatter mostly into \emph{industrial task} and \emph{high stress}. Its zero
recall is an honest consequence of the class being one recording session rather than a
generalizable physiological state, and it should be read as such rather than as a
failure of the features.

Figure~\ref{fig:subject} reveals the second main finding: per-operator macro-F1 varies
widely---from 0.15 (barely above the 0.2 chance floor for a five-class problem) to
0.78 across the 52 operators---so a detector that works for one operator can be
near-useless for another. This variance, not the mean, is the practical obstacle to
assistive deployment, and it motivates the subject-personalization discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{The generalization gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} and Figure~\ref{fig:gap} contrast the within-subject ceiling with
the \ac{LOSO} result on identical features. The within-subject classifiers reach
$0.91$ macro-F1 (XGBoost 0.910, HistGB 0.911), against $0.45$--$0.46$ across operators
for the same models---a gap of roughly $0.45$ points. This gap is the headline finding
of the paper and the reason row-level evaluations of physiological windows are
misleading: most of the apparent accuracy of a row-level split reflects
\emph{recognizing the person}, not the task state. An assistance system deployed on a
new operator without any calibration data should expect performance near the lower,
\ac{LOSO} numbers, not the within-subject ones.

\subsection{Does fusion help?}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} answer the modality question
under the same protocol: for each of the leading models we run \ac{EEG}-only (96-d),
peripheral-only (32-d), and fused (128-d) inputs. Peripheral physiology alone already
beats \ac{EEG} alone by a wide margin (e.g.\ \ac{XGBoost}: $0.39$ vs.\ $0.33$), yet
adding \ac{EEG} on top of the periphery still helps: fused improves over the best
single modality for all four models, by $+0.072$ (\ac{XGBoost}, $p<10^{-4}$),
$+0.050$ (HistGB, $p = 0.002$), $+0.047$ (RF, $p = 0.015$), and $+0.037$ (MLP,
$p = 0.105$, n.s.). The two-tower network, which fuses through separate modality
towers rather than concatenation, reaches $0.406 \pm 0.158$, above fused \ac{MLP} and
every single-modality input but significantly below fused \ac{XGBoost}
($-0.056$, $p = 0.001$). This result matters for the fusion literature: on this data,
the gains from combining central and peripheral physiology are real and significant
for the leading models, but they are an order of magnitude smaller than the
operator-effect dominating the error budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:significance} reports the per-fold paired Wilcoxon comparison of every

\subsection{Real-time feasibility}

The sensor windows are on the order of tens of seconds, while all evaluated detectors
classify a single window in roughly 2--90\,$\mu$s of CPU time---about
5\,$\mu$s for the best detector and 19\,$\mu$s for \ac{XGBoost}
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), i.e., orders of magnitude faster than real time on
commodity CPUs. Real-time adaptive assistance is therefore not constrained by
inference cost; it is constrained by the reliability question of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the honest numbers mean}

The central message of this paper is that task-state recognition from physiological
signals, evaluated across operators, is genuinely hard: the best detector reaches
$0.466$ macro-F1 under \ac{LOSO}, while the same features and a row-level split
report $0.91$ (a number we reproduce with a stratified within-subject split and then
explain as leakage of operator identity). We consider the \ac{LOSO} figure the only
defensible headline, and we recommend that physiological-recognition papers report
the within-subject ceiling and the across-operator result \emph{together}, as in
Table~\ref{tab:gap}; reporting either alone is misleading in opposite directions.

\subsection{Why recognition is hard here}

Three factors combine. (i) \emph{Operator effect dominates}: physiological baselines
and gains differ so much between people that the model cannot separate "this operator
is stressed" from "this operator responds strongly" without calibration data
\cite{aygun2022assistive,borghi2025operatorstress}. (ii) \emph{Coarse but confusable
classes}: cognitive-load battery and industrial-task conditions plausibly share
attentional and motor components, so confusion between them is physiologically
reasonable. (iii) \emph{Extreme imbalance} on the last two classes makes them
statistically near-impossible to learn from 224 and 71 windows spread over few
conditions. None of these is fixable by a better classifier alone.

\subsection{Comparison with the source protocol}

Bussolan et al.\ report subject-disjoint recognition of \emph{self-reported} stress
and cognitive-load levels with macro-F1 around 0.34--0.49
\cite{bussolan2025multiphysiohrc,bussolan2024stressdetector}. Our numbers are not
directly comparable for two reasons: their targets come from STAI-Y1 / NASA-TLX
self-reports on a subset of conditions, whereas ours are deterministic task-condition
labels on all conditions; and their protocol concerns a narrower assembly setting.
Obtaining the dataset's questionnaire tables (which are not in the public feature
export we used) would let us map our task-condition predictions onto the
self-report levels and compare head-to-head; we flag this as the single most valuable
extension rather than claim comparability we cannot substantiate.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Construct validity of labels.} Our labels are task conditions, not
measured mental states. A cognitive-load condition may not produce cognitive load in
every operator at every window; the label is a \emph{protocol} label. We state this
explicitly and never equate a prediction with a diagnosis.
\item \textbf{Class-size and condition confound.} The \emph{other} class is a single
condition (71 windows); a detector can effectively memorize that condition. Its
recall is reported only for transparency.
\item \textbf{Within-condition autocorrelation.} Even under \ac{LOSO}, a test fold
contains consecutive windows of the same condition from one operator; per-fold scores
are per-operator estimates, not independent samples. Our paired tests respect this by
pairing per-operator scores. The pooling step (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) is
descriptive, not an independence claim.
\item \textbf{Feature provenance.} We cleaned the exported feature table we obtained;
we did not recompute features from raw signals. The manifest makes the cleaning
reproducible, but the upstream feature computation is the dataset authors'.
\item \textbf{Sensor placement and recording hardware} are fixed by the dataset and
were not varied; results may not transfer to different hardware or real factory
noise, where motion artefacts would be larger than in a laboratory \ac{HRC} setting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deployment considerations}

Physiological sensing in the workplace raises privacy and acceptability questions
that are not technicalities. \ac{EEG} headsets and physiological wearables capture
information far beyond task state, and continuous monitoring of stress in industrial
settings can be used for worker surveillance rather than assistance. Any deployment
should (i) process signals on-device where feasible and retain only task-state
decisions, (ii) use predictions to \emph{adapt robot behaviour}, never for individual
performance evaluation, and (iii) make the operator's right to opt out and to know
what is inferred explicit. A failure/fallback policy is equally important: because
confidence is currently poorly calibrated (Section~\ref{sec:results}), assistance
must degrade gracefully (fall back to a fixed, safe robot policy) whenever the
detector's confidence is low, rather than acting on a low-confidence state estimate.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a leakage-free, subject-disjoint benchmark of task-state recognition on
the public MultiPhysio-\ac{HRC} dataset. After removing recording metadata and
duplicated \ac{EEG} features, eleven detectors and one explicit-fusion network were
evaluated over all 52 operators with preprocessing refit inside each fold. The honest
headline is modest---best across-operator macro-F1 of $0.466$---and our main
contribution is precisely this honest picture: a quantified generalization gap of
about $0.45$ points between within-subject and across-operator performance, a
small but detectable fusion benefit, wide per-operator variance, and uneven
calibration.
These are the properties an assistance system actually faces, and they explain why
optimistic row-level evaluations of physiological windows do not transfer to new
operators.

Future work will pursue three directions. (i) \emph{Personalization}: given the
operator effect, few-shot calibration---a handful of labelled windows from a new
operator, either as adaptation data or as a per-operator prior---is the most direct
route to usable accuracy, and our per-operator score files make the potential gain
measurable. (ii) \emph{Self-report targets}: obtaining the dataset's questionnaire
tables would let us bridge the task-condition schema to the STAI/NASA-TLX
stress and cognitive-load levels used by the source authors, enabling the direct
comparison we currently flag as out of scope. (iii) \emph{Temporal context}: the
present work classifies windows independently; modelling the condition sequence and
per-operator dynamics with recurrent or state-space models is a natural next step now
that a clean, leakage-free feature set and a fair protocol exist. The reproducibility
package (cleaning protocol, feature manifest, per-fold results, scripts) is released
with the paper.
